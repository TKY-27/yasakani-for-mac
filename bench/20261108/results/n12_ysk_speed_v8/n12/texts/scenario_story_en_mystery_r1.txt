The wind did not howl; it screamed. It was a high-pitched, thin sound that seemed to vibrate against the glass of the dome, a constant reminder that the observatory sat on the precipice of the world, three thousand meters above the tree line. Inside, the silence was absolute, broken only by the hum of the backup generator kicking in with a shudder that rattled the coffee cups on the workbench.

Elias Thorne, the observatory’s chief calibration engineer, stood in the center of the main control room. He was a man of precise angles and quiet movements, his hands usually steady enough to adjust a lens by a fraction of a millimeter. Tonight, however, his hands were trembling. The power had failed twenty minutes ago. The primary systems were dead. The backup generator was sputtering, its light flickering in a rhythm that felt less like electricity and more like a dying heartbeat.

"Is he dead?" asked Dr. Aris Thorne, Elias’s brother and the observatory’s director. Aris was a man of broad shoulders and loud opinions, currently pacing the length of the room with the energy of a caged animal.

Elias did not look up from the console. "No," he said, his voice flat. "He is alive. He is unconscious. But he is not dead."

Aris stopped pacing. He stared at the figure slumped in the chair by the telescope’s control panel. It was Julian Vane, the observatory’s resident astrophysicist, a man who had been obsessed with a specific anomaly in the data for the last six months. Julian’s head was tilted back, his eyes closed, his skin pale in the dim emergency lighting.

"Then why is the door locked?" Aris asked. "And why is the window sealed? And why is the alarm system showing a breach at 23:42, when we were all in the briefing room?"

Elias finally looked up. His eyes were dark, tired, and sharp. "Because someone wanted us to think he was dead. Or worse, that he had committed suicide. But Julian doesn’t kill himself. He doesn’t even kill his houseplants. He talks to them."

The room was a locked room in the truest sense. The main door was a heavy steel slab, magnetically sealed from the inside. The only other exit was the service hatch in the ceiling, which required a manual release from the outside, and the large observation window, which was a single pane of reinforced glass, bolted shut from the interior. There were no hidden passages. The observatory was a box of steel and glass, and Julian was inside it.

"Who else is here?" Elias asked.

"Me, you, Julian, and Sarah," Aris said. "Sarah is in the lower lab. She’s been working on the spectral analysis. She hasn’t come up."

Elias nodded. "Then we have a problem. If Julian is alive, and the room is locked, and the alarm indicates a breach, then someone entered this room, did something to Julian, and left. But they couldn’t have left through the door or the window. So they must have left through the ceiling hatch. But the hatch is sealed from the outside. Unless..."

"Unless they didn’t leave," Aris said. "Unless they are still here."

Elias looked around the room. There were only three people in the room: himself, Aris, and the unconscious Julian.

"Then one of us is the killer," Elias said.

**Part II: The Warm Brass Key**

Elias moved to the console. He was not a detective by trade, but he was a man who understood systems. A system was a set of rules, and when a system failed, it was because a rule had been broken. He began to inspect the room, not for blood or weapons, but for inconsistencies.

The first thing he noticed was the temperature. The room was cold, the heating system having failed with the power. But the console was warm. Not from the electronics, which were dead, but from a residual heat that seemed to radiate from the surface.

He placed his hand on the console. It was warm to the touch.

"Strange," he murmured.

Aris looked over. "What’s strange?"

"The console. It’s warm. The power has been off for twenty minutes. The electronics should have cooled down. But this is still warm. Like it was recently used."

Aris frowned. "Maybe the backup generator is keeping it warm."

"No," Elias said. "The generator is in the basement. The heat wouldn’t reach here. This is localized heat. Something was running here recently. Something that generates heat."

Elias looked at the console again. He saw a small, brass key lying on the surface. It was not a standard key. It was a brass key, shaped like a star, with a small, intricate cut. It was warm.

Elias picked it up. It was hot to the touch, as if it had been held in someone’s hand for a long time.

"A key," he said. "A brass key. It’s warm. Someone has been holding it recently."

Aris looked at the key. "What does it open?"

Elias turned the key over in his hand. It was small, delicate. It didn’t look like it could open a door. It looked like it could open a lock. A small lock.

"There’s a lock on the telescope’s control panel," Elias said. "A small, brass lock. It’s used to secure the data drives. It’s not a security lock. It’s a calibration lock. It’s used to prevent accidental changes to the telescope’s settings."

Aris stared at the key. "So someone used this key to unlock the control panel. And then they left it here."

"Yes," Elias said. "And they left it warm. Which means they were holding it recently. Which means they were in this room recently."

Aris looked at Julian. "So someone came in here, unlocked the control panel, did something to Julian, and left. But they couldn’t have left. So they are still here."

Elias looked at Aris. "Or they left through the ceiling hatch. But the hatch is sealed from the outside. Unless they sealed it from the inside."

Aris frowned. "How can you seal a hatch from the inside?"

Elias looked up at the ceiling. The service hatch was a small, circular opening, covered by a metal plate. It was sealed with a magnetic lock. The lock was on the outside. But there was a manual release on the inside. A small, brass lever.

Elias looked at the brass key in his hand. It was not a key for the hatch. It was a key for the control panel.

But it was warm.
