The storm took the power at 3:41 a.m., and by 4:12 the Verna Ridge Observatory was a black box on a black mountain.

Mara Voss heard the first crack of the outage from the calibration lab, a low mechanical shudder that ran down the wall where the instrument racks were bolted. Then the emergency lights came on, red and bloodless, painting the aisles in the color of fresh meat. She looked at the power bus display, saw the main breaker icon blink twice and go dark, and felt the same chill that had seeped through the observatory’s triple glass even with the mountain below her in a white sheet of cloud.

She was the calibration engineer for the new adaptive-optics commissioning team. There were eleven of them on the mountain, all of them younger than she was, all of them convinced that a storm could not stop science. They had argued in the control room before the outage. They had argued again after it, because the emergency batteries were holding out and the instruments were still alive in a reduced mode.

Then the head astronomer went into the sealed dome room and did not come back.

The dome room was the oldest part of the observatory, a circular steel-and-brass chamber at the top of the main telescope before the modern control hall had been built around it. Its single door was a heavy internal door with a keyed deadbolt and a magnetic latch. When the mains failed, the magnetic latch held. The door could be opened only with the brass key, and the brass key, as usual, was in the lock.

By the time the emergency generator finally began, the door was locked, the key was in the lock, and Dr. Ivo Hahn was slumped over the calibration bench inside.

They did not call him dead until much later, and that was the first mistake. When the first responders forced the door by driving a wedge under it and prying the deadbolt back, they found Hahn on the bench, one hand closed around a brass key, the other resting on a voice recorder. His eyes were open but not seeing. His lips were blue. He did not answer. For a long moment in the red emergency light, it looked as though the observatory had produced a locked-room murder after all: the victim in a sealed room, the key inside, no one in or out.

Mara stood outside the forced door and watched them lower Hahn onto a stretcher.

“Apparent respiratory arrest,” the paramedic said. “Maybe exposure, maybe something worse. He’s cold, but there’s a pulse. Barely.”

Mara frowned. “Why is he in the dome room?”

“Because that’s where he said he was working.”

The director, a tall man named Corbin, was already on the radio with the valley below. The storm was still up there. The access road was buried. No one was coming up in the next six hours.

Mara took the brass key from the lock.

It was warm.

# II. A Key Held in the Hand

The dome room smelled of cold metal, tea, and something faintly chemical: the odor of isopropyl alcohol from the lens-cleaning station. Emergency lights made everything look as if the room had been drained of color. The telescope, a two-meter beast wrapped in canvas, stood at the center like a sleeping animal. Around it were the usual trappings of a working instrument: calibration cards, a motorized mirror, a rack of lenses, a small heater that had been switched off, and a table where someone had left the tools of their trade.

On the bench lay the voice recorder, its little screen dark. Beside it, a cup of tea sat in a dented mug. The tea was still in the mug, untouched, and it had a bitter, dry sheen that marked it as unsweetened. Mara knew that from experience. Hahn’s tea was always unsweetened. It was one of the few facts about him that the whole team knew without asking.

She put on gloves and touched the brass key.

It was warm all over, not just on the surface. Brass holds heat well, but not for long. In a room that had been unheated for hours, a key that had been lying still would be cold as the mountain. This key had been held or heated within minutes.

“Who had it?” she asked.

The paramedics looked at each other. Corbin said, “It was in the lock. We forced the door. That’s how we got in.”

“No,” Mara said. “That’s not how it got in.”

She pointed to the lock. The deadbolt had been retracted by the wedge. The key was not in the lock. It had been in Hahn’s hand.

They found a second key at the bottom of his coat pocket. It was cold.

That was worse than the warm key would have been.

“Two keys,” Corbin said.

Mara nodded. “One in his pocket. One in his hand. If he was alone, and if the room was really locked from inside, why are there two?”

No one answered. The emergency generator coughed outside, and the red lights flickered once.

Hahn was moved to the infirmary room on the lower level. The paramedics began the work of bringing him back: warm blankets, oxygen, careful checks. Mara stayed in the dome room. She wanted the silence. The silence was part of the evidence.

The star chart on the wall caught her eye.

It was a large chart, printed on matte paper, the kind used for visual navigation. It showed the ridge as seen from the telescope’s field of view: a band of stars with labels in thin black type. Except that the chart was upside down. The constellation names were inverted, and the horizon line ran across the top of the page instead of the bottom.

Someone had printed it wrong. Or someone had hung it wrong. Or someone had done it on purpose.

Mara looked at the chart for a long time, then at the telescope, then at the recorder on the bench.

She turned on the recorder.

It did not start.

She held the power button. The little screen lit for a second, then went dark. She tried again. The recorder powered on, but only for a moment. The battery was dead, or almost dead. She opened the battery door, found the cell still inside, and removed it. It was cold.

She would need a fresh cell to see what the recorder had kept.

The first fresh cells in the lab were in a sealed drawer, and the drawer was locked.

Not locked with a key. Locked with a number.

Mara did not know the number. She was not supposed to. The calibration team had a shared code, and the emergency drawer was part of a separate protocol. But she was also the only person on the mountain who had been trained to open the drawer without a key, using a mechanical override in the cabinet frame. She had done it once before, when the code was changed and no one could remember it.

She worked the override quietly, a small brass pin sliding in a slot, and the drawer opened.

Inside were batteries, fuses, spare lamps, and a voice recorder with a full cell already installed. She took that recorder, set it on the bench where Hahn’s had been, and played the original file from Hahn’s unit once it had a fresh battery.

It started with the sound of rain on the dome.

Then a voice.

“I’m calibrating the guide star. The room is sealed. If I don’t come out, check the logs.”

Silence.

A click.

Eleven seconds of nothing.

Then the voice again, slower, hoarse:

“Something’s wrong with the key. The lock is jammed. I can’t get it back out.”

Another click.

The recorder ended.

Mara played it three times.

Eleven seconds.

The silence was not the absence of sound. It was the absence of evidence.
