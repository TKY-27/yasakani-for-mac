**Part I: The Silence of the Summit**

The wind did not howl; it screamed. It was a high-pitched, thin sound that vibrated against the thick glass of the dome, a constant reminder that the Kessler Peak Observatory sat three thousand feet above the treeline, isolated by a single, winding gravel road that had been closed by the first snow of the season.

Inside, the silence was heavier. The main power grid had failed at 19:00, plunging the facility into a darkness that was only broken by the emergency backup lights—dim, amber pools of illumination that cast long, distorted shadows across the control room. The hum of the servers, usually a comforting white noise, had died. The only sound now was the rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of the cooling fans spinning down and the distant, muffled roar of the storm outside.

Elias Thorne, the observatory’s lead calibration engineer, stood by the main console. He was a man of precise habits and quiet demeanor, his fingers stained with the faint, oily residue of grease and solder. He was not a detective by trade, but he was the only person in the building who understood the intricate, mechanical heart of the facility. When the lights flickered and died, he had been the first to check the breakers, the second to check the generator, and the third to realize that the generator had been manually disconnected.

"Dr. Aris?" Elias called out, his voice sounding small in the vast, circular room.

No answer.

Elias moved toward the heavy steel door that led to the observation deck. The door was locked from the inside. He could see the handle, a heavy brass lever, but it did not move. He pressed his ear against the cold metal. Silence.

"Dr. Aris, the power is out. The heating is off. You need to come out."

Still nothing.

Elias checked his watch. 19:15. The temperature inside the dome was already dropping. The observatory was designed to withstand the elements, but it was not designed to be a prison. If Aris was in there, he was trapped. If he was not in there, then who had locked the door?

Elias looked around the control room. It was a sparse space, dominated by the massive, dormant telescope and the banks of dead monitors. On the central desk, a single object caught the amber light: a brass key. It was warm to the touch, radiating a heat that seemed impossible given the ambient temperature. Elias picked it up. It was the master key to the observation deck. It should have been in Aris’s pocket.

**Part II: The Upside-Down Sky**

Elias set the key on the desk and turned back to the door. He tried the handle again. It was solid. He looked for a window. There was none. The observation deck was a sealed environment, accessible only through this door and a small, reinforced hatch in the ceiling that required a specialized tool to open. The hatch was closed.

He was trapped in the control room, and Aris was trapped in the deck. Or so it seemed.

Elias began to pace. His mind, trained to find anomalies in data, began to scan the room for inconsistencies. The power failure was the first anomaly. The generator had been disconnected, but the breaker switch was in the "on" position. This meant someone had physically pulled the plug from the wall socket.

The second anomaly was the key. Why was it warm? Elias held it up to the light. The brass was polished, but there was a smudge of something dark on the tip. He brought it closer to his nose. It smelled of ozone and burnt plastic.

He looked at the desk again. Next to the key lay a star chart. It was a large, laminated sheet, pinned to the desk with a single thumbtack. Elias frowned. The chart was printed upside down. The constellations were inverted, the North Star at the bottom, the horizon line at the top.

Aris was a meticulous astronomer. He would never leave a chart upside down. It was a sign of distress, or perhaps a message. Elias picked up the chart. The paper was cool, but the ink was fresh. He looked at the date printed in the corner: *October 14, 2023*. Today was October 15.

The chart was from yesterday.

Elias’s heart rate increased. He looked at the voice recorder on the desk. It was a small, black device, the kind used for logging observations. The red light was off. He pressed the play button.

Static. Then, a voice.

*"…calibration is off by two degrees. The mirror alignment is… I can’t see the… help…"*

The recording cut off abruptly. Elias checked the timestamp. The file was 45 seconds long. But when he listened to the end, there was a gap. Eleven seconds of silence where there should have been sound.

Eleven seconds.

Elias looked at the door again. The handle was still locked. He looked at the key in his hand. It was still warm.

**Part III: The Missing Seconds**

Elias sat down at the desk, his mind racing. He was a calibration engineer. His job was to ensure that the telescope’s mirrors were aligned to a precision of microns. He understood systems. He understood cause and effect.

The power failure was not an accident. The generator was disconnected. The key was in the control room. The chart was upside down. The recording had a gap.

Who was in the observation deck?

Elias looked at the door. He tried to open it again. It was locked. He looked at the ceiling. The hatch was closed. He looked at the floor. There was a small, circular vent near the base of the door. It was too small for a person, but large enough for a cable.

Elias looked at the voice recorder again. He played the recording again.

*"…calibration is off by two degrees. The mirror alignment is… I can’t see the… help…"*

The gap. Eleven seconds.

Elias closed his eyes. He listened to the silence. In the silence, he heard the wind. He heard the tick of the fans. He heard his own breathing.

He opened his eyes. He looked at the key. He looked at the chart. He looked at the door.

He realized he had been thinking about this wrong. He had been assuming that Aris was in the observation deck. But what if Aris was not in the observation deck?

What if Aris was in the control room?

Elias looked around. The room was empty. But the door was locked from the inside. If Aris was in the control room, how was the door locked?

It wasn’t.

Elias looked at the handle. It was a lever, not a knob. It could be locked from the outside with a key. But the key was in the control room.

Unless…

Elias looked at the key again. It was warm. He had been holding it for ten minutes. It should have cooled down. But it was still warm.

He looked at the desk. There was a cup of tea. It was unsweetened. The liquid was dark, and there was no steam rising from it. It was cold.

Elias picked up the cup. It was empty.

He looked at the key. He looked at the chart. He looked at the door.

He realized the truth.

The door was not locked.

It was jammed.

**Part IV: The Jammed Door**

Elias approached the door. He pushed on the handle. It did not move. He pulled. It did not move.

He looked at the gap between the door and the frame. It was tight, but not sealed. He could see a sliver of light from the observation deck.

He looked at the key. He inserted it into the lock. It turned.

The door did not open.

Elias frowned. He tried again. The key turned, but the door did not move.

He looked at the gap again. He saw something. A thin, dark line running along the bottom of the door.

He crouched down. He shone his flashlight into the gap.

He saw a wire.

A thin, copper wire, wrapped around the bottom of the door frame. It was connected to a small, black box that was hidden behind the baseboard.

Elias’s mind raced. The wire was connected to the lock mechanism. It was a simple, mechanical lock. But it had been tampered with.

The wire was pulling the lock closed.

Elias looked at the box. It was a small, electronic device. It had a battery and a small motor.

He realized what had happened.

Someone had used the wire to jam the door. They had used the motor to pull the lock closed. They had used the key to turn the lock, but the wire had prevented the door from opening.

It was a trick. A physical trick.

Elias looked at the key. It was warm because it had been used recently. The heat was from the friction of the lock.

He looked at the chart. It was upside down because it had been dropped.

He looked at the voice recorder. The eleven seconds were missing because the recording had been stopped.

He looked at the cup of tea. It was cold because it had been left for a long time.

He looked at the door.

He was not trapped.

He was not in the control room.

He was in the observation deck.
