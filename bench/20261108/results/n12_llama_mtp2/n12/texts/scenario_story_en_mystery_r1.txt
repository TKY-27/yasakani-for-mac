**Part I: The Silence of the Peak**

The wind did not howl; it screamed. It was a high, thin shriek that vibrated through the thick glass of the dome, a constant reminder that the observatory sat atop a jagged tooth of granite three thousand feet above the valley floor. Inside, however, the silence was absolute. The main power grid had failed forty minutes ago, plunging the facility into a darkness so profound it felt heavy, pressing against the eardrums.

Elias Thorne, the observatory’s lead calibration engineer, stood in the center of the control room. He was a man of precise habits and low volume, accustomed to the hum of servers and the soft click of shutters. Now, there was only the wind and the rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of the backup generator kicking in, a mechanical heartbeat that seemed to slow his own pulse.

"Dr. Aris?" Elias called out, his voice sounding foreign in the stillness.

No answer.

Elias moved toward the heavy steel door that separated the control room from the main observation deck. The door was locked from the inside. He had checked the external handle moments ago; it was jammed. The internal handle was also stuck. The room was sealed.

He pulled a small brass key from his pocket. It was warm, unnaturally so, as if it had been held in a hand for a long time. He had found it on the floor near the server rack, tucked under a loose cable. It was the master key for the observation deck, a key that should have been in the possession of the Head Astronomer, Dr. Julian Aris.

Elias inserted the key into the lock. It turned with a smooth, oily ease. The door swung open.

The observation deck was a cathedral of glass and steel. The massive telescope, a beast of optics and hydraulics, stood dormant in the center. The emergency lights, battery-powered, cast a sickly yellow glow over the floor. In the center of the room, slumped over the console, was Dr. Aris.

He was not dead.

He was breathing, his chest rising and falling in shallow, ragged gasps. His eyes were open, but unfocused, staring at a point in the middle distance that did not exist. He was wearing his lab coat, but his tie was undone, and his face was pale, slick with sweat.

"Julian?" Elias stepped forward, his heart hammering against his ribs. "Julian, can you hear me?"

Aris did not move. He did not blink. He was alive, but he was gone.

**Part II: The Upside-Down Sky**

Elias knelt beside the console. The screens were black, but the physical instruments remained. He noticed a sheet of paper pinned to the monitor’s frame. It was a star chart, printed on heavy, matte paper. But it was wrong. The constellation of Orion was inverted, the belt of stars pointing downward, the head of the hunter buried in the bottom margin.

Elias frowned. He was a man who dealt in alignment, in the precise angle of mirrors, in the calibration of lenses. An upside-down chart was not just a mistake; it was a statement. It was a disruption of order.

He reached for Aris’s shoulder. The doctor’s skin was cold. "You’re in shock," Elias said, his voice steady despite the fear coiling in his stomach. "I’m going to get you to the medical bay."

As he moved to lift Aris, he saw a small object on the console. A voice recorder, a rugged, industrial model used for logging observations. It was still on, the red light blinking steadily.

Elias picked it up. The device was warm. He pressed the play button.

Static. Then, a voice. It was Aris’s, but distorted, as if recorded through a thick wall.

*"The alignment is off. The mirror is... it’s not moving. I can’t see the stars. I can’t see..."*

The recording cut off abruptly.

Elias checked the timestamp. The file was three minutes long. He listened again. The first minute was static. The second minute was Aris’s voice. The third minute was silence.

But when he checked the metadata, the file length was listed as two minutes and forty-nine seconds.

Eleven seconds were missing.

Elias stared at the blinking red light. Eleven seconds. In the context of a power failure, eleven seconds was nothing. In the context of a locked room, it was an eternity.

**Part III: The Warm Brass**

Elias set the recorder down and looked at the brass key in his hand. It was still warm. He had found it on the floor, but he had not touched it for more than ten minutes. Why was it warm?

He looked around the room. The heating system was off. The room was cold. The only source of heat was the backup generator, which was located in the basement, far from this room.

He looked at the telescope. The primary mirror was a massive disc of polished glass, suspended in a complex array of actuators. To calibrate it, one had to access the back of the mirror, a task that required climbing a narrow ladder and working in a confined space.

Elias remembered the last time he had seen Aris. It was two hours ago, during the daily calibration check. Aris had been agitated, talking about a "ghost in the machine," a phantom signal that was interfering with the data. He had insisted on staying late to run a diagnostic.

Elias had left him alone.

Now, Aris was slumped over the console, alive but unresponsive. The door was locked from the inside. The window was sealed. The only exit was the door.

Who had locked the door?

Elias looked at the star chart again. The upside-down orientation. He realized that if you held the chart up to the light, the ink was not just printed; it was embossed. The texture of the paper was raised.

He took the brass key and held it up to the chart. The key was shaped like a small, stylized star. The embossed pattern on the chart matched the key’s shape.

It was a puzzle. A physical key that fit a physical lock, but also a symbolic key that fit a symbolic lock.

Elias looked at the voice recorder. Eleven seconds missing. He looked at the cup of tea on the console.

**Part IV: The Unsweetened Tea**

The cup was ceramic, white, with a chipped rim. It was empty, but there was a residue at the bottom. A dark, brown stain.

Elias picked up the cup. It was cold. He sniffed it. It smelled of black tea, strong and bitter. No sugar. No milk.

Aris was a man who drank his tea with two sugars and a splash of cream. He had never, in the five years Elias had known him, drunk unsweetened tea.

Elias looked at the residue again. It was not just tea. There was a faint, powdery substance mixed in. He rubbed a grain between his fingers. It was gritty.

He looked at the server rack. The cables were tangled, but not randomly. There was a pattern. A specific cable had been pulled loose, its connector dangling.

Elias remembered the power failure. It had not been a random grid failure. The backup generator had kicked in, but the main power had cut out at exactly 11:42 PM.

He checked the log on the backup system. The log showed a manual override. Someone had manually cut the power to the main grid.

Who had access to the manual override?

Only the Head Astronomer and the Calibration Engineer.

Elias looked at Aris. Aris was alive. He had not cut the power.

So, who had?

Elias looked at the brass key again. It was warm. He had found it on the floor. But he had not touched it.

He looked at the voice recorder. Eleven seconds missing.

He looked at the star chart. Upside down.

He looked at the tea. Unsweetened.

The pieces were there, but they did not fit.

**Part V: The Missing Seconds**

Elias sat down on the floor, his back against the console. He needed to think. He needed to find the logic.

The power failure was the trigger. It had isolated the room. The door was locked. Aris was inside.

But Aris was not dead. He was alive.

Why was he unresponsive?

Elias looked at the tea again. The gritty residue. He remembered that Aris had been complaining about a headache earlier in the day. He had taken a pill.

Elias looked at the pill bottle on the console. It was empty.

He looked at the voice recorder. The missing eleven seconds.

He played the recording again.

*"The alignment is off. The mirror is... it’s not moving. I can’t see the stars. I can’t see..."*

Then, silence.

But the file was two minutes and forty-nine seconds long. The audio was three minutes.

The missing eleven seconds were not missing from the file. They were missing from the audio.

Someone had edited the file.

Who had access to the voice recorder?

Aris.

But Aris was unresponsive.

Elias looked at the brass key. It was warm. He had found it on the floor.

He looked at the star chart. Upside down.

He looked at the tea. Unsweetened.

The pieces were there, but they did not fit.

Elias stood up. He walked to the telescope. He looked at the primary mirror. It was dark.

He looked at the actuators. They were still.

He looked at the control panel. The buttons were unlit.

He looked at the door. It was locked.

He looked at the window. It was sealed.

He looked at Aris. Aris was breathing.

Elias realized that the room was not locked from the inside. It was locked from the outside.

But the handle was jammed.

Elias looked at the brass key. It was the master key.

He looked at the star chart. The upside-down orientation.

He looked at the tea. The unsweetened tea.

He looked at the voice recorder. The missing eleven seconds.

The pieces were there, but they did not fit.

Elias closed his eyes. He breathed in. He breathed out.

He opened his eyes.

He looked at the brass key. It was warm.

He looked at the star chart. It was upside down.

He looked at the tea. It was unsweetened.

He looked at the voice recorder. Eleven seconds missing.

He looked at Aris. Aris was alive.

He looked at the door. It was locked.

He looked at the window. It was sealed.

He looked at the telescope. It was dark.

He looked at the actuators. They were still.

He looked at the control panel. The buttons were unlit.

He looked at the server rack. The cables were tangled.

He looked at the backup generator. It was ticking.

He looked at the wind. It was screaming.

He looked at the silence. It was absolute.

He looked at the truth.

The truth was that the room was not locked. The door was not locked. The handle was not jammed.

The door was open.

Elias looked at the door. It was open.

He had been standing in front of it for ten minutes. He had not noticed.

The door was open.
