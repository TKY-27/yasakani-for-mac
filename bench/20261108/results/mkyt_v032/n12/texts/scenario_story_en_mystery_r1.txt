**Part I: The Silence of the Summit**

The wind did not howl; it screamed. It was a high-pitched, tearing sound that vibrated against the thick glass of the Aerie Peak Observatory, a structure bolted to the granite lip of the mountain like a barnacle on a hull. Inside, the silence was absolute, save for the rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of the backup generator kicking in, a mechanical heartbeat that replaced the hum of the main grid.

Elias Thorne, the observatory’s lead calibration engineer, stood in the center of the main dome. He was a man composed of sharp angles and quiet habits, his fingers perpetually stained with the faint residue of lubricant and graphite. He had been alone for three days, a routine isolation period required to recalibrate the primary mirror’s tracking servos. The power failure had occurred at 02:00, a sudden drop that plunged the facility into darkness before the emergency lights flickered on, casting long, distorted shadows across the floor.

When the lights stabilized, Elias found Dr. Aris Vane slumped over the central console. Vane, the observatory’s director and a man who treated the stars as personal property, was unconscious. His face was pale, his lips slightly blue. The room was locked. The heavy steel door was sealed from the inside with a manual deadbolt, and the only other exit was a service hatch that required a key to open from the outside. The windows were sealed shut against the altitude.

Elias checked Vane’s pulse. It was weak but present. "Aris," he whispered, shaking the man’s shoulder. "Aris, wake up."

Vane groaned, his eyes fluttering open. He looked at Elias, then at the ceiling, then back at Elias. "The door," Vane rasped. "I locked it. I was working on the logs. I didn’t move."

Elias looked at the door. The deadbolt was engaged. The key was in the lock. "You’re alive," Elias said, his voice steady, though his heart hammered against his ribs. "That’s the only thing that matters right now."

**Part II: The Warm Brass**

Elias pulled a chair from the corner and sat, keeping his distance from Vane, who was now sitting up, rubbing his temples. The air in the dome was cold, but the console was warm. Elias noticed something on the edge of the desk, near Vane’s hand. It was a small, brass key, no larger than a thumb. It was warm to the touch, radiating a heat that seemed impossible in the frigid room.

"Where did this come from?" Elias asked, picking it up. The metal was smooth, polished by use.

Vane stared at it, his confusion deepening. "I don’t know. I was reviewing the spectral data. I didn’t touch anything."

Elias turned the key over in his fingers. It was not the key to the main door; that was a heavy, iron mechanism. This was a smaller, more delicate key. He looked at the service hatch. The lock on the hatch was a standard brass cylinder.

"Did you use the service hatch?" Elias asked.

"No," Vane said. "I never do. It’s a fire exit. I locked the main door to ensure no one could interrupt the calibration sequence. I was alone."

Elias placed the warm key on the desk. "If you didn’t use it, and you’re alive, and the room is locked, then someone else was here. Or, someone else is here."

Vane’s eyes widened. "There’s no one else. The staff left two days ago. I’m alone."

Elias stood up and walked to the door. He checked the hinges. They were solid, bolted to the frame. He checked the window seals. Intact. He looked at the ventilation shafts. They were too small for a human.

"Then we have a problem," Elias said. "Because this key is warm, Aris. It’s been held, or it’s been in a warm place, for the last few minutes. And you’re saying you didn’t touch it."

**Part III: The Upside-Down Sky**

Elias moved to the console, where a stack of papers lay scattered. He picked up the top sheet. It was a star chart, printed on heavy, matte paper. But it was wrong. The constellations were inverted. Orion was upside down. The North Star was at the bottom of the page.

Vane looked at it and frowned. "That’s impossible. I printed this this morning. I checked it twice. It was right-side up."

Elias held the chart up to the light. The ink was fresh, still slightly tacky. "If you printed it this morning, and it was right-side up, and now it’s upside down, then someone turned it over. Or, someone printed it upside down and you didn’t notice."

"I would have noticed," Vane said. "I’m a director, not a child."

Elias looked at the printer. It was a high-end laser model, connected to the main computer. The power failure had cut the main grid, but the printer had a battery backup for emergency jobs. "Did you print anything else?"

"No. Just this chart. I needed it for the alignment check."

Elias looked at the chart again. The inversion wasn’t random. The stars were mirrored. It was a specific type of error, one that occurred if the paper was fed into the printer backwards. "Aris, did you feed the paper in backwards?"

"No. I’m careful."

Elias nodded slowly. "Then someone else fed it in. And they did it while you were unconscious. Or, while you were distracted."

Vane looked at Elias, his fear palpable. "Who would do that? Why?"

"I don’t know," Elias said. "But it’s a clue. Someone wanted you to see this chart. Or, someone wanted to hide something in the way it was printed."

**Part IV: The Missing Seconds**

Elias moved to the voice recorder, a small, black device sitting on the desk. It was a standard dictaphone, used for logging observations. The power failure had not affected it, as it ran on batteries.

Elias pressed the play button. The recording began. It was Vane’s voice, calm and measured. "Log entry, 01:45. Checking servo alignment. All systems nominal. The wind is picking up. I’m going to review the star chart for the final calibration."

The recording stopped.

Elias frowned. He pressed play again. The same words. The same stop.

He checked the timestamp. The recording started at 01:45:00 and ended at 01:45:11. Eleven seconds.

"That’s not enough," Elias said. "I was in the room for at least ten minutes before the power failed. I heard the generator kick in. I heard the wind. I didn’t hear anything else."

Vane looked at the recorder. "I was talking. I was saying I was going to review the chart. Then I felt dizzy. I sat down. I don’t remember anything after that."

Elias looked at the eleven seconds. "Eleven seconds is a long time. In eleven seconds, you can walk across the room. You can pick up a key. You can turn a page. You can do a lot of things."

"Who was here?" Vane asked.

"I don’t know," Elias said. "But the recorder stopped. It didn’t pause. It stopped. That means someone pressed the stop button. Or, the battery died. But the battery is full."

Elias looked at the recorder. The stop button was depressed. "Someone pressed stop. And they did it quickly. They didn’t wait for the recording to end naturally. They cut it off."

**Part V: The Unsweetened Tea**

Elias looked at the cup on the desk. It was a ceramic mug, white and simple. Inside, there was a dark liquid. It was tea.

Elias picked up the mug. It was warm. He brought it to his nose. It smelled of black tea, no milk, no sugar.

"Aris, did you drink this?"

Vane looked at the cup. "I don’t drink tea. I drink coffee. And I don’t drink it unsweetened. It’s too bitter."

Elias looked at the cup. "Then who drank it?"

Vane’s face went pale. "I don’t know. I don’t know who was here."

Elias set the cup down. "The tea is warm. That means it was made recently. And it was drunk. Or, it was left to cool. But it’s still warm. That means it was made within the last hour."

Elias looked at the room. The door was locked. The windows were sealed. The service hatch was closed.

"Then someone was here," Elias said. "And they made tea. And they drank it. Or, they left it for you."

Vane looked at Elias, his eyes wide. "For me? Why would someone leave me tea?"

"I don’t know," Elias said. "But it’s a clue. Someone wanted you to have something to drink. Or, someone wanted to leave a trace."

He looked at the upside-down star chart. It had been printed backwards. That meant someone had fed the paper in backwards.

He looked at the voice recorder. Eleven seconds were missing. That meant someone had pressed the stop button.

He looked at the tea. It was unsweetened. That meant it was not for Vane.
