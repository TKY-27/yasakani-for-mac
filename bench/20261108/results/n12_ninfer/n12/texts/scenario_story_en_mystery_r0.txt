**Part I: The Silence of the Summit**

The wind did not howl at the Kestrel Peak Observatory; it screamed. It was a high-pitched, metallic shriek that vibrated through the reinforced glass of the dome, a sound that had been the background noise of Elias Thorne’s life for the past six months. But tonight, the scream was punctuated by a sudden, violent silence.

The lights died. Not just the overhead fluorescents, but the emergency backups, the red standby indicators on the server racks, and the soft blue glow of the main telescope’s control panel. The darkness was absolute, save for the faint, rhythmic pulse of the moonlight filtering through the thick, curved glass above.

Elias froze, his hand hovering over a wrench. He was not an astronomer. He was a calibration engineer, a man who dealt in microns, torque specifications, and the precise alignment of mirrors. His job was to ensure the machine saw what was there, not to interpret what it saw. He stood in the center of the control room, the cold air biting at his exposed forearms, listening to the hum of the ventilation system cut out.

"Power failure," he said aloud, his voice sounding too loud in the vacuum. "Grid down."

He moved toward the heavy steel door that led to the corridor. It was locked from the outside, a standard security protocol during storm events to prevent unauthorized access to the sensitive equipment. The keycard reader was dead. The manual override handle was stiff, frozen by the sub-zero temperatures creeping in from the unheated outer walls.

Then, he heard it. A thud. Soft, muffled, but distinct. It came from the observation deck, behind him.

Elias spun around. The room was pitch black. He fumbled for his pocket, finding the small LED flashlight he kept for maintenance checks. He clicked it on. The beam cut through the dark, illuminating dust motes dancing in the still air.

At the far end of the room, near the base of the primary telescope, lay Dr. Aris Vane.

Vane was the lead researcher, a man whose ego was as large as the mountain they sat upon. He was sprawled on the floor, his face turned away, one arm flung out. He was wearing his lab coat, which was now stained with a dark liquid.

Elias rushed forward, his heart hammering against his ribs. "Dr. Vane?"

No response. The body was still.

Elias checked for a pulse. Nothing. He looked closer. The stain on the coat was not blood. It was tea. Dark, steeped, and spreading across the white fabric like a bruise.

Panic, cold and sharp, spiked in Elias’s chest. He grabbed Vane’s shoulder. The man was warm. Too warm. And then, a gasp. A wet, rattling breath.

Vane’s eyes snapped open. They were wide, dilated, and filled with a terror that transcended language. He tried to speak, but only a croak escaped his throat. He pointed a trembling finger toward the ceiling, toward the dome, and then back at Elias.

Elias looked up. The stars were visible, but the view was obstructed by a thick layer of frost forming on the inside of the glass. The temperature was dropping rapidly. Without power, the heating systems were offline. In this altitude, hypothermia would set in within hours.

But more pressing than the cold was the fact that they were trapped. The door was sealed. The phone lines were dead. The radio was silent.

And Vane was dying. Or so it seemed.

**Part II: The Weight of Brass**

Elias dragged Vane to the nearest chair, a sturdy metal stool used for adjusting the secondary mirror. He pulled his own jacket off and wrapped it around the scientist’s shoulders. Vane shivered violently, his teeth chattering.

"Who did this?" Vane whispered, his voice barely audible over the wind.

"I don't know," Elias said, keeping his voice steady. "We need to wait for the backup generator. It should kick in automatically."

"It won't," Vane said. His eyes were fixed on Elias. "I killed it. Before... before I fell."

Elias frowned. "What do you mean?"

"The breaker box," Vane wheezed. "In the utility closet. I tripped the main fuse. I wanted to isolate the system. To test the fail-safes. But I didn't expect..." He trailed off, clutching his chest.

Elias knew the layout of the observatory better than anyone. The utility closet was adjacent to the control room, accessible only through a narrow service hatch in the wall. If Vane had gone in there, he could have come back out. But if someone else was in there...

"Can you walk?" Elias asked.

"No," Vane said. "My leg. It hurts."

Elias examined Vane’s right ankle. It was swollen, purple, and clearly fractured. Vane had fallen, or been pushed, onto the edge of the raised platform where the telescope controls were located.

"We need to get you to the medical bay," Elias said. "It’s two floors down. But the elevator is dead."

"Stairs," Vane groaned. "Take me to the stairs."

Elias helped Vane stand. The weight of the man was surprising; he was heavier than he looked. As they moved toward the service hatch, Elias noticed something on the floor near where Vane had lain.

A key.

It was brass, old-fashioned, with a complex bit pattern. It wasn’t a keycard. It was a physical key, the kind used for the master lock on the external maintenance door—the one that led to the roof access ladder.

Elias picked it up. It was warm. Not hot, but warm, as if it had been held in a hand recently.

"Why is this here?" Elias asked.

Vane stared at the key. His expression shifted from fear to confusion. "That’s my key. I keep it in my pocket. Always."

"Then why is it on the floor?"

"I don't know," Vane said. "I don't remember anything after I went into the closet."

Elias pocketed the key. It felt significant. A physical object in a digital world. A tangible clue.

They reached the service hatch. Elias opened it. The air inside was colder, smelling of ozone and stale oil. The hallway beyond was dark, lit only by the faint green glow of the emergency exit signs, which were battery-powered and still functional.

As they stepped into the hallway, Elias heard a click. Then another.

Footsteps.

They stopped. The footsteps stopped.

"Who's there?" Elias called out.

Silence.

"Is it you, Aris?" Elias asked, though he knew it wasn't.

No answer.

Elias guided Vane carefully down the stairs. Each step was a negotiation between pain and endurance. By the time they reached the ground floor, Vane was pale, his lips tinged with blue.

The medical bay was a small, sterile room with a cot and a basic first-aid kit. Elias laid Vane down. He checked the supplies. Bandages, antiseptic, splints. No morphine. No advanced trauma care.

"You're going to be okay," Elias lied. "We just need to stabilize your leg."

Vane closed his eyes. "You’re the only one here, aren’t you? Besides me?"

"Yes," Elias said. "Just us."

**Part III: The Upside-Down Sky**

While Elias worked on setting Vane’s ankle, using a roll of gauze and a rigid board from the supply cabinet, his mind raced. The situation was impossible.

1.  The power failed.
2.  Vane claimed he tripped the breaker himself.
3.  Vane was found injured in the control room.
4.  The door was locked from the outside.
5.  There was no one else in the building.

Or was there?

Elias remembered the footsteps. He had heard them. He had seen the shadow of a figure in the hallway, fleeting and indistinct.

He looked at Vane. "Did you see anyone when you woke up?"

Vane shook his head weakly. "No. Just the dark. And the cold."

Elias finished the splint. It was crude, but it would hold. He needed to think. He needed data.

He walked back to the control room. The air was freezing now. His breath misted in front of him. He turned on his flashlight again.

He scanned the room. The telescope was still pointing at the sky. The star chart on the wall was illuminated by the moonlight.

Wait.

Elias squinted. The star chart was printed upside down.

He had installed that chart three weeks ago. He had aligned it perfectly with the local meridian. It was never upside down.

He approached the wall. The chart was a large, laminated sheet, pinned to the drywall. The pins were straight. The paper was flat. But the orientation was wrong. Polaris was at the bottom. The celestial equator was inverted.

Why?

Elias touched the paper. It was cold. He looked at the pins. One of them was missing. The top-left pin was gone.

He looked at the floor. There, near the base of the telescope, was a small, silver pin.

He picked it up. It was bent.

Someone had removed the pin, rotated the chart, and re-pinned it. Why?

To obscure something? To hide a specific star?

Elias looked at the chart again. With the chart upside down, the field of view for the telescope’s current position was different. If the telescope was aimed at a specific coordinate based on the *correct* chart, it would be looking at a patch of empty space. But if the operator believed the chart was correct, they would be aiming at a different location entirely.

This was a misdirection. A deliberate error.

Who would want to misdirect the telescope?

And why would they do it during a power failure?

Elias realized he was missing something. The power failure wasn’t random. Vane said he tripped the breaker. But why?

To create darkness. To hide the act of rotating the chart.

But who was the actor?

Elias thought about the people in the building. There were only four of them.

Himself.
Vane.
Sarah Jenkins, the junior researcher.
And Marcus Cole, the security guard.

Where were Sarah and Marcus?

Elias checked the time on his watch. 11:42 PM. The storm had started at 9:00 PM. The power had failed at 10:15 PM.

He had been working in the control room since 8:00 PM. He hadn’t seen Sarah or Marcus since then.

He assumed they were in their quarters. But the footsteps...

Elias went to the intercom system. It was dead. He tried the landline. Dead.

He was alone.

Or was he?

He looked at the window. The frost was thickening. He couldn’t see out.

He looked at the door. Locked.

He looked at the service hatch. Closed.

He was trapped.

And Vane was dying.

**Part IV: Eleven Seconds of Void**

Elias returned to the medical bay. Vane was awake, staring at the ceiling.

"The chart," Elias said. "It’s upside down."

Vane’s eyes widened. "What?"

"The star chart. Someone rotated it."

Vane sat up, wincing. "That’s impossible. I checked it an hour ago. It was perfect."

"Someone did it while you were unconscious," Elias said. "Or while you were in the closet."

"I didn't go into the closet!" Vane shouted, then winced. "I swear to God, I didn't."

"Then who did?"

"I don't know," Vane said. "But I know who has the motive."

"Who?"

"Marcus," Vane said. "He’s been acting strange. Aggressive. He’s been asking questions about the new sensor array. Questions he shouldn’t be asking."

"Marcus is the security guard," Elias said. "He doesn’t have access to the technical details."

"He has access to the keys," Vane said. "He carries the master key ring. And he knows the layout. He knows where the breakers are. He knows how to disable the alarms."

Elias considered this. Marcus was a big man, quiet, efficient. He had been hired six months ago. He had no known connections to the research team.

"Why would Marcus want to rotate the chart?" Elias asked.

"To steal data," Vane said. "The new sensor array is capable of detecting exoplanets with unprecedented accuracy. The data is worth millions. If Marcus can copy the raw data before it’s uploaded to the central server, he can sell it to private investors. Or worse, to foreign governments."

"But the power is off," Elias said. "The servers are down. He can’t copy anything."

"Not yet," Vane said. "But he can prepare. He can mark the files. He can set up a script to run when the power comes back on. And if the chart is wrong, the telescope will aim at the wrong place. The data will be corrupted. Unusable. And no one will notice until it’s too late."

Elias nodded. It made sense. A sabotage job.

But it didn’t explain the injury. Why was Vane broken?

"Did Marcus hit you?" Elias asked.

"I don't know," Vane said. "I was in the closet. I heard a noise. I came out. And then... I fell."

"Or you were pushed."

Vane looked at Elias. "You think Marcus pushed me?"

"I think someone wanted to stop you from seeing what happened," Elias said.

"What happened?"

"The rotation of the chart."

Vane shook his head. "I didn't see anything. I was disoriented."

Elias needed proof. He needed to find the person who did this.

He looked at the voice recorder on the desk in the control room. It was a standard dictaphone, used for logging observations. It was always recording.

If the power failed, the recorder would have stopped. But if it was battery-powered...

Elias ran back to the control room. He grabbed the recorder. He pressed play.

Static.

Then, a voice.

*"The alignment is complete. The target is locked. We begin the sequence in ten seconds."*

It was Vane’s voice.

Then, silence.

Eleven seconds of static.

Then, a crash.

And then, Vane’s voice again, pained. *"Oh god. My leg."*

Elias rewound the tape. He listened again.

The eleven seconds of silence.

What happened in those eleven seconds?

A footstep? A shout? A struggle?

Nothing.

Just static.

Unless...

Elias looked at the recorder. The battery indicator was low.

He checked the timestamp. The recording started at 10:14 PM. The power failed at 10:15 PM.

The eleven seconds of silence corresponded exactly to the moment the power failed.

But why was there no sound?

Because the microphone was muted.

Someone had muted the microphone.

Who had access to the recorder?

Only the researchers.

Vane.
Sarah.
And Elias.

Elias looked at the recorder. The mute button was depressed.

He had not pressed it.

Vane had not pressed it.

So Sarah must have.

But Sarah was in her quarters.

Was she?

Elias left the control room. He walked to the hallway. The emergency lights cast long, distorted shadows.

He went to Sarah’s door. He knocked.

No answer.

He tried the handle. It was locked.

He kicked the door. It didn’t budge.

He tried the window. It was barred.

Sarah was inside.

Or she wasn’t.

Elias went to the next door. Marcus’s quarters.

He knocked.

"Marcus?"

No answer.

He tried the handle. It was unlocked.

He pushed the door open.

The room was empty.

The bed was made. The clothes were folded.

But the window was open.

The wind howled in, whipping the curtains.

Marcus was gone.

**Part V: The Unsweetened Tea**

Elias stood in the doorway, the cold wind hitting his face. He looked out. The night was dark, the stars hidden by clouds.

He looked back into the room. On the desk, there was a cup.

Tea.

Unsweetened.

It was still warm. Steam rose from it in thin wisps.

Marcus drank unsweetened tea. Vane drank sweetened tea. Sarah drank coffee.

This was Marcus’s cup.

And it was warm.

Which meant Marcus had been here recently.

But the window was open.

Had Marcus fled?

Or had he been taken?

Elias picked up the cup. He smelled it. Black tea. Strong.

He looked at the floor. There were footprints in the dust.

They led from the door to the window.

And then, they stopped.

There were no footprints leading out the window.

Which meant Marcus had not jumped.

Which meant Marcus was still in the room.

Or he had vanished.

Elias searched the room. Under the bed. Behind the wardrobe. In the bathroom.

Nothing.

He went back to the hallway. He checked Sarah’s door again.

This time, he heard a sound.

A whisper.

From inside.

"Help me."

It was Sarah’s voice.

Elias broke the lock with a crowbar from the tool shed. He burst into the room.

Sarah was sitting on the floor, tied to a chair. Her mouth was covered with tape. She was terrified.

Elias cut the tape off her mouth.

"Thank God," she gasped. "I’ve been here for an hour. I don’t know where Marcus is. I don’t know where Vane is."

"Vane is in the medical bay," Elias said. "He’s alive. But he’s hurt."

"Who did this?" Sarah asked.

"I don't know," Elias said. "But I think we’re not alone."

He looked at the window. It was closed.

He looked at the door. It was open.

He looked at the corner of the room.

A figure was standing there.

It was Marcus.

He was holding a gun.
