The wind outside the Aerie Peak Observatory did not howl; it screamed. It was a high-pitched, metallic shriek that vibrated through the thick glass of the dome, a sound that had become so constant over the last decade that Dr. Aris Thorne had stopped hearing it and started feeling it in his teeth.

Aris was not a detective by trade. He was a calibration engineer for the National Optical Astronomy Research Association (NOARA). His job was to ensure that the mirrors of the Great Eye telescope were aligned to within nanometers, that the sensors were cool enough to detect the faintest whispers of light from the edge of the universe, and that the software didn’t crash when the temperature dropped below zero. He was a man of precision, of clean lines and sterile environments. He did not deal with blood, or lies, or the messy, chaotic variables of human emotion.

But tonight, the power was out.

The backup generators had failed three hours ago, likely due to the ice buildup on the intake vents. The observatory was plunged into a darkness so profound it felt heavy, pressing against the skin. The only light came from the emergency strips along the floor—dim, amber LEDs that cast long, distorted shadows—and the faint, pulsing glow of the server room’s status lights, which were blinking red in a rhythmic, arrhythmic panic.

Aris found him in the control room.

Or rather, he found *him*. Julian Vane, the observatory’s director, was slumped over the main console. His head rested on his arms, his face hidden. To an untrained eye, it looked like sleep. To Aris, who knew the specific posture of exhaustion that plagued every astronomer during a data crunch, it looked like death.

"Julian?" Aris whispered.

No response.

Aris moved closer, his boots squeaking on the polished concrete. He reached out to touch Julian’s shoulder. The fabric of the lab coat was cold, but beneath it, there was heat. Not the feverish heat of illness, but the residual warmth of a body that had been active moments before.

Julian stirred. He lifted his head, his eyes wide and wet, staring at Aris with a mixture of terror and confusion.

"Aris," Julian gasped, his voice raspy. "I... I don't know what happened."

Aris pulled back, his heart hammering against his ribs. "You’re alive. You’re okay."

"I’m locked in," Julian said, looking around the room. The door to the control room was sealed. The keypad was dead, the screen black. The window was a solid pane of reinforced glass, bolted shut. There were no other exits. The control room was a steel-and-glass box, suspended two thousand feet above the valley floor.

"How did you get in here?" Aris asked, though he already knew the answer. They had been working together until the power cut. Aris had left to check the generator logs in the basement. When he returned, the doors had locked automatically—a safety protocol triggered by the total system failure.

"I was calibrating the primary mirror," Julian said, rubbing his temples. "I heard a noise. Then... nothing. I woke up here. I’ve been trying to open the door for twenty minutes. My hands are shaking too badly to type the code."

Aris nodded slowly. He was a man of logic. If the door was locked, and they were inside, then no one else could be in the room. Unless someone was hiding. But the room was small, filled with racks of servers and monitors. There was nowhere to hide.

And yet, something was wrong.

The air smelled stale, but underneath the ozone scent of the electronics, there was another smell. Earthy. Bitter. Tea.

Aris looked at the desk. On the corner, near Julian’s elbow, sat a ceramic mug. It was white, chipped at the rim. Inside, the liquid was dark, still, and steaming slightly.

"Did you drink this?" Aris asked.

Julian stared at the cup as if it were a venomous snake. "I don’t remember drinking anything. I don’t even like tea. I hate the taste. It’s bitter. Unsweetened."

Aris picked up the cup. It was warm. Not hot, but warm. Enough to suggest it had been poured recently. Maybe ten minutes ago. Maybe less.

If Julian hadn’t drunk it, who had? And why leave it there?

Aris set the cup down carefully. He needed to think. He needed to look at the room not as a scientist, but as a detective. He began to walk around the perimeter of the control room, his eyes scanning every surface.

First, he noticed the star chart.

It was pinned to the wall behind the main console, a large, laminated map of the northern sky. It was used for quick reference during observations. But as Aris looked at it, he frowned. The constellations were inverted. Orion was upside down. The Big Dipper was hanging from the ceiling.

"Julian," Aris said. "Look at the chart."

Julian squinted. "What? Why is it upside down?"

"It wasn’t upside down when I left," Aris said. "I checked it an hour ago. I use it to verify the telescope’s pointing coordinates."

Julian shrugged, a nervous gesture. "Maybe I turned it over while I was... confused. I don’t know."

Aris shook his head. People don’t accidentally pin a chart upside down. That takes intent. Or, at least, a deliberate act of disorientation. Why would someone invert a star chart? To confuse? To signal?

He moved to the next clue. On the floor, near the base of the server rack, lay a brass key.

It was an old-fashioned key, the kind used for physical locks, not digital ones. It was warm to the touch. Aris picked it up. The metal was smooth, worn by years of use. It fit the lock on the supply closet in the hallway, the one that held the spare fuses and the emergency rations.

"Why is this here?" Aris asked.

"I don’t know," Julian said. "I didn’t bring it in."

Aris examined the key. There was a smudge of grease on the bow. It was fresh. Someone had handled it recently. Someone had used it.

But the supply closet was in the hallway, outside the control room. If the door was locked, how did the key get in here?

Unless the person who brought it in was already inside.

Aris looked at Julian again. Julian was pale, sweating. He was trembling. But he was also watching Aris with a sharp, intelligent gaze. He wasn’t just a victim. He was a participant.

"The recorder," Aris said suddenly.

On the desk, next to the tea, was a digital voice recorder. It was a standard model, used for logging observations. The display showed a timestamp: 02:14 AM.

Aris pressed the play button.

Static. Then, silence.

He fast-forwarded. The file was supposed to be a continuous log of their conversation. But as he listened, he realized something was missing.

The audio jumped. One second, Julian was saying, "The mirror alignment is off by two microns." The next second, Aris was saying, "Let’s recalibrate."

There was a gap. Eleven seconds of silence.

Eleven seconds where nothing was recorded.

Aris frowned. Digital recorders don’t skip unless the battery dies or the memory card fails. But the battery was full. The memory card was intact.

Who deleted eleven seconds? And why?

Aris looked at Julian. "Do you know what happened in those eleven seconds?"

Julian closed his eyes. "I don’t know. I don’t remember."

"You’re lying," Aris said softly.

Julian opened his eyes. "I’m not lying. I’m scared. I don’t know what happened to me. I woke up here, and I don’t know how I got here."

Aris studied him. Julian was a brilliant astronomer, but he was also a difficult man. He had a history of erratic behavior, of pushing people away, of isolating himself. He had been under immense pressure to secure funding for the new telescope upgrade. If the project failed, the observatory would be shut down.

Aris looked at the clues again.

The warm brass key.
The upside-down star chart.
The eleven seconds of missing audio.
The unsweetened tea.

They were all connected.

The tea was the first thing. Julian hated unsweetened tea. But he drank it. Why? Because it was the only thing available. Or because he was forced to drink it.

The key. It was warm. It had been used recently. But the supply closet was outside. Unless...

Aris looked at the door again. The keypad was dead. But the physical handle was still functional. The electronic lock was bypassed, but the mechanical latch was still engaged.

Wait.

Aris walked to the door. He tried the handle. It turned. The door opened.

He stepped out into the hallway. The emergency lights were dim, casting long shadows. The hallway was empty.

He looked at the supply closet. The door was open.

Inside, the shelves were bare. The fuses were gone. The rations were gone.

But on the floor, near the threshold, was a trail of footprints. Wet footprints.

Aris followed them. They led back to the control room.

He went back inside. Julian was still sitting at the desk, staring at the wall.

"Julian," Aris said. "When you woke up, where were you?"

"On the floor," Julian said. "Near the door."

"And the tea?"

"I was holding it."

Aris nodded. "And the key?"

"In my pocket."

Aris looked at the upside-down star chart. "Why is it upside down?"

Julian hesitated. "Because I was dizzy. I couldn’t tell right from wrong."

Aris shook his head. "That’s not it. You inverted it to hide something."

"Hide what?"

"The time."

Aris pointed to the clock on the wall. It was digital, powered by the emergency battery. It read 02:35 AM.

"The recorder says 02:14 AM," Aris said. "That’s twenty-one minutes ago. But the tea is still warm. The key is still warm. That means the event happened recently. Within the last five minutes."

Julian’s face drained of color. "What are you saying?"

"I’m saying that you didn’t wake up twenty minutes ago. You woke up five minutes ago. And you’ve been manipulating the evidence to make it look like you were unconscious for a longer period."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because you wanted to cover up what you did."

Aris walked to the desk. He picked up the recorder. "Those eleven seconds. You deleted them. You used the admin password to edit the file. You removed the part where you admitted to doing something."

"What did I admit to?" Julian whispered.

"To poisoning the tea."

Julian stared at him. "I didn’t poison the tea."

"No," Aris said. "You didn’t. But you made it look like you did. You inverted the star chart to create a sense of chaos. You left the key to suggest that someone broke in. You deleted the audio to hide your own actions."

"But what actions?"

Aris looked at the cup. "You didn’t poison the tea. You poisoned yourself."

Julian blinked. "What?"

"You took a sedative. A strong one. One that causes amnesia. You took it to escape the pressure. You wanted to disappear for a few hours. You locked yourself in the control room, knowing the power would fail. You planned to wake up after the sedative wore off, and then you would claim that you had been unconscious. That someone had attacked you. That you had been drugged."

"But why?"

"Because you were going to be fired. The board was meeting tomorrow. They were going to vote to shut down the observatory. You needed a reason to stay. You needed a crisis. You needed to be a victim."

Aris looked at the warm tea. "You drank the tea to mask the taste of the sedative. You inverted the chart to show that you were disoriented. You left the key to suggest a break-in. And you deleted the audio to remove any trace of your own planning."

Julian sat in silence. His hands were still trembling. But now, the tremble was different. It was the tremble of shame.

"I didn’t want to hurt anyone," Julian said quietly. "I just wanted to buy some time. I thought... I thought if I disappeared for a night, they would see that I was indispensable. That without me, the observatory would fail."

"You almost killed yourself," Aris said. "Sedatives can be fatal, especially in high doses. And if you had woken up later, you would have been found dead. Or worse, you would have been found in a state of delirium, and they would have committed you."

Julian looked at the cup. "I’m sorry."

Aris felt a surge of anger. Not at Julian, but at the situation. At the pressure that drove a brilliant man to such desperate measures. At the bureaucracy that valued profit over science.

"What happens now?" Julian asked.

"Now, we wait for the power to come back. We report what happened. And you go home."

Julian nodded. "And the observatory?"

Aris looked out the window. The stars were visible, bright and cold. The Great Eye telescope stood silent in the dark, its mirror reflecting the faint light of the moon.

"We’ll see," Aris said.

He picked up the cup of tea. It was still warm. He looked at it for a long moment. Then, he poured it out into the sink.

He washed the cup. He dried it. He put it back on the desk.

Then, he straightened the star chart. He pinned it correctly, Orion upright, the Big Dipper in its proper place.

He picked up the brass key. He placed it in Julian’s hand.

"Keep it," Aris said. "For luck."

Julian looked at the key, then at Aris. "Thank you."

Aris nodded. He turned off the recorder. The silence in the room was heavy, but it was a peaceful silence.

They sat in the dark, waiting for the dawn.
