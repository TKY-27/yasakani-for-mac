**Part I: The Silence of the Dome**

The silence was not empty; it was heavy, a physical weight that pressed against the eardrums. In the observatory, silence was usually the presence of the machinery—the hum of the cooling fans, the whir of the tracking motors, the low thrum of the power grid. Now, there was only the wind, howling against the reinforced glass of the dome, and the rhythmic, terrified tapping of Dr. Elias Thorne’s fingers on the mahogany desk.

I stood in the center of the room, my boots crunching slightly on the cold tile floor. My name is Julian Vane, and I am not a police officer. I am a calibration engineer, specializing in high-precision optical systems. I had been hired by the Alpine Institute for Astrophysics to recalibrate the primary mirror of the 4-meter telescope, a job that required me to stay at the remote station for a week. I was here for the math, for the geometry of light, not for the drama.

But the drama had arrived with the blackout.

Ten minutes ago, the lights had died. The backup generators, which were supposed to kick in within three seconds, had failed. The emergency lights flickered once, then died, leaving us in a darkness so profound it felt like being submerged in ink. In that darkness, I heard a scream. It was a sharp, jagged sound, cut short by a heavy thud. When the emergency lights finally sputtered back to life, casting a sickly, amber glow over the room, Dr. Thorne was slumped over his desk, his face buried in a stack of spectral data prints.

"Dr. Thorne?" I called out, my voice sounding too loud in the small, circular room.

He didn’t move.

I approached him slowly. The room was a locked-room scenario in the truest sense. The single door to the observatory was bolted from the inside. The only other exit was a service hatch in the floor, which was sealed with a heavy iron latch that required a specific key to open. The windows were high up, made of bulletproof glass, and the dome itself was closed and locked. We were alone.

I reached out to touch his shoulder. He jerked upright, gasping for air, his eyes wide and dilated. He was alive. He was shaking, his hands trembling so violently he couldn’t grip the pen he had been holding.

"Who are you?" he whispered, his voice cracking. "What did you do?"

I stepped back, raising my hands. "I didn’t do anything. I was in the control room. I heard the scream."

Thorne looked at me with a mixture of terror and confusion. He looked at the door, then at the floor hatch, and then back at me. "The door was locked. I locked it myself. I always lock it during observations. No one could have entered. No one could have left."

"And yet," I said, my engineer’s mind already turning the gears of logic, "you are alive, and you are injured. And someone else is in this room."

Thorne pointed a shaking finger at the corner of the room. "The key. Look at the key."

**Part II: The Warm Brass**

I followed his gaze. On the floor, near the base of the telescope’s pedestal, lay a brass key. It was an old-fashioned, heavy key, the kind used for the service hatch. It was warm to the touch.

I picked it up. The metal was heated, not by the sun—there was no sun here—but by a hand. It had been held recently.

"Where did this come from?" I asked.

Thorne’s eyes were fixed on the key, not on me. "It was in my pocket. I keep it there. I always keep it there."

I examined the key. It was a standard cut, but the bow was ornate, engraved with the Institute’s crest. I turned it over in my fingers. The warmth was dissipating, but it was still distinct. If the key had been in the floor for long, it would have been cold, matching the ambient temperature of the room, which was roughly ten degrees Celsius. The fact that it was warm meant it had been held within the last few minutes.

"I didn’t drop it," Thorne said, his voice gaining a fraction of its former authority, though it was still brittle. "I was working. I was analyzing the data from the Andromeda sector. I didn’t move. I didn’t stand up."

I looked at the desk. There were papers scattered across it, but they were not disheveled. They were arranged in a precise grid. The only thing out of place was a single sheet of paper, a star chart, which lay face down on the desk.

I picked up the chart. It was a high-resolution printout of the constellation Cygnus. But it was printed upside down. The stars were inverted, the nebulae reversed.

"Why is this upside down?" I asked.

Thorne flinched. "I don’t know. I don’t know what’s happening. I feel... I feel like I’m being watched. I felt a presence. I felt a hand on my shoulder. I screamed because I was afraid. But when I turned, no one was there."

I looked around the room again. The telescope dominated the center, a massive eye of polished steel and glass. The control panels were dark, their screens black. The air smelled of ozone and old paper.

"Let’s start from the beginning," I said. "When did the power go out?"

"About ten minutes ago," Thorne said. "I was alone. I was working. Then the lights went out. I heard a noise. A scuffle, maybe. I felt a blow to the back of my head. I fell. I tried to get up, but I was dizzy. I heard the door open. I saw a shadow. Then the lights came back on, and I was on the floor."

"The key," I said. "You said it was in your pocket."

"Yes."

"And now it is on the floor. Warm."

Thorne nodded slowly. "I must have dropped it when I fell."

"Did you fall from the chair?"

"I was standing. I was pacing. I was agitated."

I looked at the key again. If Thorne had dropped it while standing, it would have hit the floor with force. The brass would have been cold if it had been on the floor for ten minutes. But it was warm.

"Who else has access to this room?" I asked.

"No one. I am the only one here. The staff is at the base. The students are in the dorms. I am alone."

"Then who is the shadow you saw?"

Thorne looked at me, his eyes wide. "I don’t know. But I know I didn’t drop the key. I know I didn’t fall. I know I was standing. And I know the door was locked."

I looked at the star chart. It was upside down. I turned it over. The stars were reversed.

"Dr. Thorne," I said. "This chart is not just upside down. It is a mirror image."

He frowned. "What?"

"Look at the label for the star Deneb. It is reversed. The letters are backwards. This chart was not printed upside down. It was printed as a mirror image."

Thorne stared at the chart. "Why would anyone do that?"

"Because," I said, "it is a clue. Or a trap."

**Part III: The Missing Seconds**

I moved to the control panel. The power was still out, but the voice recorder was battery-operated. It was a small, black device, clipped to the edge of the desk. It had a red light, which was blinking.

"Is this recording?" I asked.

Thorne nodded. "I record my observations. It’s standard procedure. I record my thoughts, my calculations, my notes."

I pressed the play button.

The static crackled. Then, Thorne’s voice filled the room.

"October 14th. 22:00 hours. The sky is clear. The wind is strong. The mirror alignment is off by 0.02 degrees. I need to adjust the tertiary mirror. I am going to make the adjustment now."

The recording stopped.

I frowned. "That was short."

"I was going to continue," Thorne said. "I was going to say what I found in the data."

I pressed play again.

The static crackled. Then, a voice. It was not Thorne’s voice. It was a man’s voice, low and calm.

"You should have left."

The recording stopped.

I looked at Thorne. He was pale. "Who is that?"

"I don’t know," he whispered. "I don’t know who that is."

I pressed play again.

The static crackled. Then, Thorne’s voice.

"Who are you? What do you want?"

The recording stopped.

I looked at the timer on the recorder. It showed 00:11. Eleven seconds.

"Eleven seconds," I said. "The recording is eleven seconds long."

Thorne nodded. "Yes."

"But," I said, "the voice you heard. The voice that said 'You should have left.' That was not in the recording."

Thorne looked at me. "What?"

"The recording is eleven seconds long. The first part is your voice, saying you are going to adjust the mirror. The second part is the man’s voice, saying 'You should have left.' The third part is your voice, asking who he is. That is eleven seconds. But you said you heard a scream. You said you felt a blow to the head. You said you saw a shadow. Those events did not happen in the recording."

Thorne’s face went white. "What are you saying?"

"I am saying that the recording does not match your story. The recording shows you talking to someone. But you said you were alone. You said you were working. You said you didn’t move. But the recording shows you interacting with someone. And the recording is missing the moment of the attack. The eleven seconds are missing. The moment when you were hit. The moment when you screamed. The moment when the key was dropped."

Thorne looked at the recorder. "That’s impossible. I was recording. I was always recording."

"Then the recorder was tampered with," I said. "Someone deleted the eleven seconds. They deleted the moment of the attack. They deleted the moment when the key was dropped. They deleted the moment when the door was opened."

Thorne looked at me. "Who?"

I looked at the key. It was still warm. I looked at the star chart. It was a mirror image. I looked at the cup of tea on the desk. It was unsweetened.

"Dr. Thorne," I said. "Who else knows about the recording?"

"No one."

"Who else knows about the mirror image?"

"No one."

"Who else knows about the tea?"

Thorne looked at the cup. "I don’t understand."

"The tea is unsweetened," I said. "You take your tea with sugar. I saw you add sugar to your tea this morning. But this cup has no sugar. The tea is unsweetened. Someone else made this tea. Someone else put it on the desk."

Thorne looked at the cup. "I... I don’t remember making this tea."

"Then someone else made it. And they left it here. And they left the key. And they left the chart. And they deleted the eleven seconds."

Thorne looked at me. "Why?"

"Because they wanted you to think you were alone. They wanted you to think you were crazy. They wanted you to think you were hallucinating. They wanted you to be afraid. And they wanted you to stay in this room. Locked in. With no one to help you."

Thorne looked at the door. "But I locked the door."

"Yes," I said. "You locked the door. But you didn’t lock the hatch."

Thorne looked at the floor. "The hatch is sealed."

"Yes. But the key is warm. The key was held recently. The key was used recently. The hatch was opened recently."

Thorne looked at me. "Who?"

I looked at the telescope. The telescope was the only other object in the room. The telescope was the only other thing that could have moved.

"Dr. Thorne," I said. "Look at the telescope."

Thorne looked at the telescope. "What?"

"The telescope is moving."

Thorne looked at the telescope. It was not moving. It was still.

"It’s not moving," he said.

"It is," I said. "Look at the shadow."

Thorne looked at the shadow of the telescope on the wall. The shadow was moving. It was moving very slowly. It was moving in a circle.

"The telescope is tracking a star," I said. "But the power is out. The telescope should be off. The telescope should be still. But it is moving. It is tracking a star that is not in the sky."

Thorne looked at the telescope. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that the telescope is being controlled. Someone is controlling it. Someone is in the dome. Someone is on the other side of the glass."

Thorne looked at the dome. The glass was dark. The sky was black.

"Who?" he whispered.

I looked at the key. I looked at the chart. I looked at the tea. I looked at the recorder.

"I don’t know," I said. "But I know that you are not alone. And I know that you are not crazy. And I know that someone is in this room. And I know that they are waiting for you to make a mistake."

**Part IV: The Geometry of Light**

I moved to the telescope. The massive eye of the instrument was silent, but the shadow on the wall told a different story. The shadow was drifting, a slow, imperceptible crawl that defied the stillness of the dead machine.

"Calibration," I murmured, more to myself than to Thorne. "If the motor is off, the shadow shouldn't move. Unless..."

"Unless what?" Thorne asked, his voice barely audible.

"Unless the mirror is being tilted manually. Or unless the entire platform is rotating."

I approached the base of the telescope. The platform was a massive ring of steel, heavy and bolted to the floor. It was designed to rotate on a set of bearings, driven by electric motors. Without power, it should be immovable.

I placed my hand on the side of the platform. It was cold. But the shadow was moving.

I looked up at the dome. The glass was curved, a perfect hemisphere. The light from the emergency lamps was casting long, distorted shadows.

"Dr. Thorne," I said. "When you said you felt a presence. A hand on your shoulder. Where were you standing?"

Thorne pointed to the desk. "Here. Right here."

I looked at the floor. The tile was cold, but there was a faint, circular stain near the desk. It was dry, but it had a different texture than the rest of the floor. It was a ring of dust, disturbed.

"You were standing here," I said. "And the telescope was over there."

"Yes."

"If you were standing here, and the telescope was over there, and the shadow is moving... then the telescope is not moving. The light is moving."

Thorne looked at me. "The light is moving?"

"The emergency lights," I said. "They are not fixed. They are on a track. They are moving."

I looked up at the ceiling. The emergency lights were small, recessed units. They were not moving.

"No," I said. "The lights are not moving. The mirror is moving."

I looked at the primary mirror. It was a huge, concave disc of polished glass. It was reflecting the emergency lights. The reflection was moving.

"The mirror is being tilted," I said. "Someone is tilting the mirror. They are using the mirror to reflect the light. They are using the light to create a shadow. They are using the shadow to move the telescope."

Thorne looked at the mirror. "How?"

"By using the light as a lever," I said. "The mirror is a lever. The light is the force. The shadow is the result. They are using the physics of the room to move the telescope. They are using the geometry of light to create a ghost."

Thorne looked at me. "Who is doing this?"

I looked at the key. I looked at the chart. I looked at the tea.

"The person who made the tea," I said. "The person who printed the chart. The person who deleted the eleven seconds."

Thorne looked at the door. "But the door is locked."

"Yes," I said. "The door is locked. But the hatch is not."

I looked at the floor hatch. It was a square of steel, set into the floor. It was sealed with a latch. The latch was locked.

"But the key is warm," I said. "The key was used. The hatch was opened."

Thorne looked at the hatch. "But I didn’t open it."

"No," I said. "You didn’t open it. But someone else did."

I moved to the hatch. I looked at the latch. It was a simple mechanism. A pin, a spring, and a lever.

"I can open it," I said.

"Wait," Thorne said. "If you open it, they will see you."

"They are already here," I said. "They are in the room. They are in the dome. They are in the mirror."

I looked at the mirror. The reflection was moving. The shadow was moving.

"Open it," I said.

I reached for the key. I inserted it into the latch. I turned it.

The latch clicked. The hatch opened.

A cold rush of air came from below. It smelled of damp earth and metal.

I looked down into the hatch. It was a small shaft, leading down into the earth. The walls were concrete. The floor was dark.

"Who is down there?" I asked.

No one answered.

I looked at Thorne. "You need to come with me."

Thorne looked at the hatch. He was afraid. But he was also curious.

"Okay," he said.

We climbed down into the shaft. The walls were cold. The air was still.
