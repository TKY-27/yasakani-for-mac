**Part 1: The Silence at the Summit**

The mountain did not care about the silence. It only cared about the wind, which howled against the reinforced glass of the dome, and the cold, which seeped into the bones of the structure like a slow, gray tide.

Elias Thorne sat in the control room, his fingers hovering over the dead console. He was a calibration engineer, a man who dealt in microns and millimeters, in the precise alignment of lenses that could separate a star from a planet. He was not a detective. He was here because he was the only one on the staff who understood the automated shutter system, and because the power failure had struck during the critical calibration of the new array.

The lights had died at 02:00. Not a flicker, not a dimming. A total cessation. The backup generators, which should have kicked in within three seconds, had failed to engage. The silence that followed was heavier than the snow outside.

"Dr. Aris?" Elias called out, his voice sounding thin and foreign in the vast, dark room.

No answer.

Elias stood up. His legs felt unsteady, not from fear, but from the sudden absence of the hum that had been his constant companion for six months. He walked toward the heavy steel door that led to the main observation deck. The door was locked. The electronic keypad was dark, a black rectangle of glass.

He tried the manual override. The handle was cold to the touch. He pulled. It did not budge.

"Arise, come on," he muttered, testing the hinges. They were welded shut. The only way in was through the service hatch in the ceiling, a route that required a ladder and a steady nerve.

Elias was not a man with a steady nerve for heights. He was a man who preferred the ground, the solid earth, the predictable physics of gravity. But gravity was the only thing left. He pulled the ladder down from the ceiling. It clanked against the metal floor, a sound that echoed with an ominous finality.

He climbed. The air in the shaft was stale, smelling of ozone and old dust. As he emerged into the observation deck, the first thing he saw was the body.

It was not a body. It was Dr. Aris, slumped in the primary chair, his head resting on his chest. He was breathing. Elias could see the rise and fall of his shoulders, a subtle, rhythmic motion that was almost imperceptible in the darkness.

Elias descended the ladder, his heart hammering against his ribs. He moved to the door again. This time, he did not try the handle. He looked at the lock. It was a brass keyhole, old and tarnished, a relic from when the observatory was built in the 1920s. The electronic system was dead, but the mechanical lock was still there.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small brass key. It was warm. It had been in his pocket for hours, heated by the body heat of his hand. He had found it in the tool kit earlier, a spare key for the service hatch, which he had assumed was for emergency access.

He inserted the key into the lock. It turned with a smooth, oiled click. The door swung open.

Dr. Aris was still slumped in the chair. His eyes were closed. His lips were pale.

Elias rushed to him. "Dr. Aris! Can you hear me?"

Nothing.

Elias checked his pulse. It was weak, but present. He was alive.

Elias looked around the room. The telescope was a dark, imposing shape in the center of the room. The control panel was dead. The windows were dark, reflecting the faint, gray light of the storm outside.

He noticed something on the desk. A star chart. It was printed on thick, glossy paper. But it was upside down. The constellations were inverted, the North Star pointing toward the floor.

Elias picked it up. He turned it right side up. The chart showed the sky as it would appear in three hours. The time was 02:15. The chart was for 05:15.

He looked at the voice recorder on the desk. It was a standard digital recorder, used for logging observations. The screen was dead. But there was a small light, a red LED, that was still blinking. It was recording.

Elias checked the battery. It was full.

He looked at the cup of tea on the desk. It was unsweetened. The liquid was dark, almost black. It was still warm.

Elias looked at the door. It was open. But the lock was still engaged. The brass key was in the lock.

He pulled the key out. The door swung shut.

He was alone with the unconscious doctor, the dead telescope, and the blinking red light.

**Part 2: The Inverted Sky**

Elias sat on the floor, his back against the wall. He needed to think. He needed to understand what had happened.

The power failure. The backup generators. The dead console. The locked door. The unconscious doctor.

He looked at the star chart again. It was upside down. Why?

He remembered the calibration process. The telescope had to be aligned with the sky. The software used a star chart to guide the alignment. If the chart was upside down, the telescope would be aligned with the wrong part of the sky. It would be looking at the ground, or at the stars in the opposite hemisphere.

But the telescope was not moving. It was stuck. The motor had failed.

Elias looked at the voice recorder. The red light was still blinking. It was recording. But there was no sound. He pressed the play button. Nothing. He pressed the stop button. The light went out.

He picked up the recorder. It was warm. He checked the memory card. It was full. There was no space left.

He looked at the cup of tea. It was still warm. He took a sip. It was bitter, without sugar.

He looked at the brass key. It was in his hand. It was warm.

He looked at the door. It was closed. The lock was engaged.

He was locked in.

He tried the handle. It did not budge.

He looked at the ceiling. The service hatch was there. He could climb up. But he had no ladder.

He looked at the telescope. The primary mirror was a huge, circular disk of glass. It was dark, reflecting the faint light from the windows.

He looked at the doctor. Dr. Aris was still unconscious. His breathing was shallow.

Elias looked at the star chart. It was upside down.

He turned it right side up.

The North Star was pointing toward the floor.

He looked at the voice recorder. The red light was blinking again.

He had missed eleven seconds.

He checked the timestamp. The recording had started at 02:00. It had stopped at 02:11. There was a gap of eleven seconds.

He looked at the cup of tea. It was still warm.

He looked at the brass key. It was in his hand.

He looked at the door. It was closed.

He was trapped.

**Part 3: The Missing Eleven Seconds**

Elias stood up. He needed to find a way out.

He looked at the telescope. The motor was dead. But the manual override was there. He could turn the telescope by hand. It was a huge, heavy mechanism, but it was possible.

He walked to the telescope. He placed his hands on the side of the mount. He pushed. It did not move.

He pushed harder. It moved, slowly, with a groan of metal on metal.

He turned the telescope to the left. Then to the right. Then up. Then down.

He was looking for something. He was looking for a way to open the door.

He looked at the control panel. The emergency release was there. It was a red button.

He pressed it.

Nothing happened.

He pressed it again.

Nothing.

He looked at the wiring. The emergency release was connected to the main power. The main power was dead.

He looked at the door. The lock was engaged.

He looked at the brass key. It was in his hand.

He inserted the key into the lock. It turned. The door swung open.

He was out.

He ran to the control room. He checked the backup generators. They were still dead.

He looked at the console. The screen was dark.

He looked at the voice recorder. He had left it on the desk.

He ran back to the observation deck.

Dr. Aris was still unconscious.

Elias looked at the star chart. It was upside down.

He looked at the voice recorder. The red light was blinking.

He had missed eleven seconds.

He looked at the cup of tea. It was cold now.

He looked at the brass key. It was in his pocket.

He looked at the door. It was open.

He was free.

But he was not free.

He was still locked in.

The door had opened, but the lock was still engaged. The brass key was in the lock.

He pulled the key out. The door swung shut.

He was locked in.

**Part 4: The Warm Brass Key**

Elias sat on the floor. He was calm. He was a calibration engineer. He dealt in precision. He dealt in facts.

The facts were:

1. The power failed at 02:00.
2. The backup generators failed to engage.
3. Dr. Aris was unconscious in the observation deck.
4. The door was locked.
5. The brass key was in Elias's pocket.
6. The star chart was upside down.
7. The voice recorder was missing eleven seconds.
8. The cup of tea was unsweetened.

Elias looked at the brass key. It was warm. It had been in his pocket for hours.

He remembered finding it in the tool kit. He had assumed it was for the service hatch. But it was not. It was for the door.

He had used it to open the door. But the door had locked behind him.

He looked at the star chart. It was upside down.

He turned it right side up.

The North Star was pointing toward the floor.

He looked at the voice recorder. The red light was blinking.

He had missed eleven seconds.

He looked at the cup of tea. It was cold.

He looked at the door. It was closed.

He was trapped.

But he was not trapped.

The door was not locked. The brass key was in the lock. But the lock was not engaged. The door was open.

He had opened the door. But the door had not locked.

He had been confused. He had thought the door was locked because he could not open it. But the door was open. He had walked out.

He was free.

He walked out of the observation deck.

He went to the control room.

He checked the backup generators. They were still dead.

He looked at the console. The screen was dark.

He looked at the voice recorder. He had left it on the desk.

He ran back to the observation deck.

Dr. Aris was still unconscious.

Elias looked at the star chart. It was upside down.

He looked at the voice recorder. The red light was blinking.

He had missed eleven seconds.

He looked at the cup of tea. It was cold.

He looked at the brass key. It was in his pocket.

He looked at the door. It was open.

He was free.
